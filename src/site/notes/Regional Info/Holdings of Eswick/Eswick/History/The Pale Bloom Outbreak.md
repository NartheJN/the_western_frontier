Several years after Eswick became an established settlement, interest in exploring the surrounding wilderness began to grow. A group of scouts and explorers travelled farther inland than they usually did, spending several weeks away from town as they mapped the region and searched for useful land and resources.

When they returned, several members of the expedition were already unwell. At first, their symptoms were mistaken for exhaustion or something they had encountered during their travels. Within days, however, pale branching marks began to appear beneath their skin, followed by strange changes to their senses and a persistent cough. By the time it became clear that the explorers had brought back an unfamiliar disease, it had already spread through much of the town.

Eswick was poorly prepared for an outbreak of this scale. The town had limited medical knowledge and few facilities for treating large numbers of sick people, making it difficult to contain the disease. The sick were isolated where possible, and a sickhouse was eventually established in an open part of town. Even so, the disease continued to spread before it finally passed, leaving a significant number of people dead or permanently affected.

The outbreak changed Eswick’s relationship with the surrounding wilderness. Exploration had been one of the main reasons the town was founded, but the disease left many residents wary of travelling too far from the settled lands. Large expeditions became uncommon, and the scouts gradually focused more on watching the frontier and dealing with nearby threats than on exploring what lay beyond it.
